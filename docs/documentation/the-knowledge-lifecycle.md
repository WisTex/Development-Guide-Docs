# The Knowledge Lifecycle

## Overview

Knowledge does not appear fully formed.

It is discovered, refined, published, shared, challenged, improved, and rediscovered over time.

Many organizations focus heavily on the lifecycle of software, products, or projects while giving little attention to the lifecycle of knowledge. As a result, valuable information is often lost, duplicated, forgotten, or left undiscovered.

Understanding how knowledge evolves is essential to effective Knowledge Management.

The Knowledge Lifecycle provides a framework for understanding how information moves from an initial observation or question into a lasting knowledge asset.

## Knowledge Is Not Static

A common misconception is that knowledge work ends when a document is published.

In reality, publication is often only the beginning.

A document may be:

* Expanded
* Corrected
* Clarified
* Reorganized
* Republished
* Referenced by other works

over many years.

Knowledge assets are living assets.

Their value often increases over time as they accumulate improvements, context, and references.

## The Knowledge Lifecycle

While every organization manages knowledge differently, most knowledge assets follow a similar lifecycle:

```text
Question
↓
Observation
↓
Idea
↓
Research
↓
Draft
↓
Review
↓
Publication
↓
Discovery
↓
Feedback
↓
Revision
```

Not every knowledge asset follows every stage, and some stages may repeat many times.

The lifecycle should be viewed as a framework rather than a rigid process.

## Question

Many knowledge assets begin with a question.

Examples include:

* How does this feature work?
* Why was this decision made?
* What problem does this solve?
* How do I perform a specific task?

Questions often reveal gaps in understanding.

These gaps create opportunities for documentation, research, and education.

## Observation

Sometimes knowledge begins with an observation rather than a question.

Examples include:

* Users repeatedly asking the same question
* Confusion during onboarding
* Missing documentation
* Common support requests
* Repeated mistakes

Observations often reveal opportunities to improve existing knowledge assets or create new ones.

## Idea

Ideas represent potential future knowledge assets.

Examples include:

* Article concepts
* Documentation improvements
* Tutorial topics
* Newsletter entries
* Glossary additions

Not every idea will become a published document.

However, preserving ideas creates opportunities for future knowledge development.

## Research

Research transforms questions and ideas into understanding.

This stage may involve:

* Reviewing existing documentation
* Interviewing subject matter experts
* Examining source material
* Testing software
* Gathering examples

Research provides the foundation for accurate and useful knowledge assets.

## Draft

Drafting transforms research into a structured form that can be reviewed and improved.

A draft may be:

* An article
* A guide
* A tutorial
* A glossary entry
* A newsletter issue
* Internal documentation

The purpose of a draft is not perfection.

The purpose is to create a version that can be evaluated and refined.

## Review

Review improves accuracy, clarity, and usefulness.

Depending on the organization, review may involve:

* Editors
* Subject matter experts
* Developers
* Project leaders
* Technical communicators

Review helps identify errors, omissions, and opportunities for improvement before publication.

## Publication

Publication makes knowledge available to its intended audience.

Examples include:

* Documentation websites
* Knowledge bases
* Wikis
* Newsletters
* Articles
* Training materials

Publication is an important milestone, but it is not the end of the lifecycle.

## Discovery

Published information only provides value if people can find it.

Discovery is often overlooked despite being one of the most important stages of the lifecycle.

Examples include:

* Search engines
* Documentation indexes
* Newsletters
* Internal search systems
* Cross-references
* Recommendations

Knowledge that cannot be discovered is often functionally equivalent to knowledge that does not exist.

## Feedback

Feedback provides insight into how knowledge is being used.

Examples include:

* Questions from readers
* Support requests
* Comments
* Corrections
* Usage analytics

Feedback reveals opportunities to improve existing knowledge assets.

## Revision

Revision keeps knowledge accurate and useful over time.

Examples include:

* Correcting errors
* Updating outdated information
* Expanding incomplete topics
* Improving clarity
* Adding examples

Most valuable knowledge assets go through multiple revision cycles throughout their lifespan.

## Knowledge Assets Are Never Truly Finished

Unlike software issues, knowledge assets rarely reach a final state.

Even mature documents may eventually require:

* Updates
* Clarifications
* Reorganization
* New examples
* Additional context

For this reason, Knowledge Management systems should treat knowledge assets as evolving resources rather than completed tasks.

## Why This Matters

Organizations that understand the Knowledge Lifecycle gain several advantages:

* Better documentation
* Improved onboarding
* Reduced support burden
* Better knowledge retention
* Increased discoverability
* More effective communication

By understanding how knowledge evolves, organizations can build systems and workflows that support long-term learning rather than simply producing documents.

## Conclusion

Knowledge is not a static resource.

It evolves through questions, observations, research, publication, discovery, feedback, and revision.

Understanding the Knowledge Lifecycle helps organizations create, preserve, and improve knowledge over time.

Effective Knowledge Management is not simply about publishing information.

It is about supporting the continuous growth, refinement, and discovery of knowledge.
