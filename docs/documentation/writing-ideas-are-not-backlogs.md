# Writing Ideas Are Not Backlogs

## Overview

Development teams often maintain backlogs.

Technical Communicators, writers, editors, and knowledge managers often maintain collections of writing ideas.

Although these collections may appear similar, they serve different purposes.

Confusing writing ideas with development backlogs can lead organizations to make poor decisions about knowledge management, documentation planning, and content creation.

Writing ideas are not backlogs.

They are opportunities.

Understanding the distinction helps organizations manage knowledge more effectively.

## What Is a Backlog?

In software development, a backlog is typically a collection of work that may be performed in the future.

Examples include:

* Features
* Enhancements
* Defects
* Technical improvements
* Infrastructure changes

Backlogs are generally expected to be:

* Prioritized
* Groomed
* Refined
* Completed
* Removed

The goal of backlog management is often to maintain a realistic and actionable collection of future work.

Large backlogs are frequently viewed as a problem that requires attention.

## What Is a Writing Idea?

A writing idea represents a potential future knowledge asset.

Examples include:

* Articles
* Tutorials
* Documentation improvements
* Glossary entries
* Newsletter topics
* Case studies
* Frequently asked questions

Unlike development work, writing ideas are often exploratory in nature.

Many may never become published content.

Others may evolve significantly before publication.

Still others may inspire entirely different knowledge assets.

## Different Goals

Backlogs and writing idea collections exist for different reasons.

A development backlog helps organize future implementation work.

A collection of writing ideas helps preserve opportunities for future knowledge creation.

One focuses on execution.

The other focuses on possibility.

## Opportunity Inventories

A useful way to think about writing ideas is as an opportunity inventory.

Each idea represents a potential opportunity to:

* Improve understanding
* Answer questions
* Reduce confusion
* Preserve knowledge
* Support users
* Document discoveries

Not every opportunity must be pursued immediately.

Some opportunities may remain valuable for years.

## Abundance Is Not a Problem

Development teams often become concerned when backlogs grow too large.

A large backlog may indicate:

* Resource constraints
* Poor prioritization
* Unrealistic planning

Writing ideas behave differently.

A large collection of writing ideas is often a sign of a healthy knowledge ecosystem.

It suggests that people are:

* Observing problems
* Asking questions
* Identifying knowledge gaps
* Discovering opportunities

The existence of many ideas does not necessarily imply that something is wrong.

## Ideas Age Differently

Development work often becomes obsolete over time.

Features are replaced.

Defects are resolved.

Infrastructure changes.

Writing ideas frequently behave differently.

A useful article idea may remain relevant for years.

An unanswered question may continue appearing long after it was first observed.

A valuable topic may become more important as a project grows.

Because of this, older ideas should not automatically be discarded.

## Not Every Idea Requires Action

One of the advantages of maintaining writing ideas separately from implementation work is that ideas can exist without creating pressure to act immediately.

An idea can be:

* Recorded
* Preserved
* Revisited later
* Combined with other ideas
* Expanded into a larger project

Its value is not dependent on immediate execution.

## The Role of Knowledge Management

Knowledge Management systems should help preserve and organize opportunities for future knowledge creation.

Examples include:

* Article ideas
* Documentation requests
* Frequently asked questions
* Research topics
* Observations
* Knowledge gaps

These opportunities often become valuable inputs into future documentation efforts.

Treating them as disposable backlog items risks losing valuable insights.

## Why This Matters

Organizations that treat writing ideas as development backlogs often make predictable mistakes.

They may:

* Delete potentially valuable ideas
* Focus only on immediate needs
* Ignore emerging topics
* Lose opportunities for future documentation

Maintaining a healthy collection of writing ideas helps ensure that valuable opportunities remain available when time, resources, and priorities align.

## Conclusion

Writing ideas and development backlogs may appear similar, but they serve different purposes.

Backlogs help manage implementation work.

Writing ideas help preserve opportunities for knowledge creation.

A backlog represents future work.

A writing idea represents future possibility.

Effective Knowledge Management recognizes the difference and treats each accordingly.
