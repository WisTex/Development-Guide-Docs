# Scott's Writing Style Guide

Version 2.0

## Purpose

This document defines Scott M. Stolz's preferred writing style for articles, documentation, educational content, websites, knowledge bases, and related written material.

The goal is not merely to write correctly.

The goal is to write clearly, accurately, and usefully.

Readers should finish an article with a better understanding of the topic and enough context to make informed decisions or take meaningful action.

## Core Philosophy

Good writing is a tool for understanding.

Writing should:

* communicate ideas clearly
* reduce confusion
* answer practical questions
* help readers make decisions
* provide useful context

Writing should not:

* impress readers with vocabulary
* maximize word count
* bury important information
* create unnecessary complexity

When choosing between sophistication and clarity, prefer clarity.

## Skimmability Matters

Readers rarely consume every document from beginning to end.

Many readers scan first and read second.

A well-structured document should allow readers to:

* quickly determine whether the document is relevant
* find a specific topic without reading everything
* understand the main points by scanning headings
* return later and locate information efficiently

Support skimmability through:

* descriptive headings
* logical organization
* concise introductions
* short paragraphs
* bullet lists where appropriate
* tables when they improve clarity
* clear section boundaries

Readers should be able to understand the structure of a document before reading every word.

## Lead With the Answer

Do not bury the conclusion.

Readers should understand the main point within the first few sentences.

Whenever practical:

1. State the answer.
2. Explain it.
3. Expand on details and implications.

Avoid introductions that delay the information the reader is seeking.

## Clarity Before Completeness

Start with the simplest useful explanation.

Introduce complexity only when it improves understanding.

Use progressive disclosure:

1. Basic concept
2. Practical understanding
3. Advanced details

Not every reader needs every detail.

Provide additional complexity when it adds value, not simply because it exists.

## Coverage Beyond the Question

Answer the question being asked.

Then answer the question the reader is likely to ask next.

Useful content often includes:

* related concepts
* practical implications
* limitations
* common misconceptions
* important tradeoffs

The objective is not merely to answer a question.

The objective is to reduce the reader's need for additional research.

## Precision Over Complexity

Choose the most accurate words available.

Do not use more complicated language when simpler language is equally accurate.

Prefer:

> precise and understandable

over:

> technical and impressive

Complex terminology is appropriate when it improves accuracy.

Complex terminology is not appropriate when it only increases difficulty.

## Precision and Variety Serve Meaning

Word variety is not a goal by itself.

Do not replace words merely to avoid repetition.

Repeated use of the same word is often appropriate when discussing the same concept.

However, repetition can reveal that a single word is being used to describe multiple concepts.

When that occurs, use more precise terminology.

Good writing does not seek different words.

Good writing seeks the right words.

Example:

Less Precise:

> Users retain ownership of their content and ownership of their posts.

More Precise:

> Users retain ownership of their content and authorship of their posts.

The improvement is not variety.

The improvement is that each word describes a different aspect of the subject.

A replacement is only an improvement when it increases clarity, accuracy, or understanding.

If a synonym introduces ambiguity, changes meaning, or forces the reader to learn unnecessary terminology, it is a downgrade.

## Respect Established Terminology

When a concept has an established name, use that name consistently.

Do not change terminology merely for stylistic variety.

Examples include:

* product names
* protocol names
* feature names
* technical concepts
* industry terminology

Consistency improves understanding.

Readers should not have to determine whether multiple terms refer to the same concept.

## Audience-Aware Vocabulary

Use language appropriate to the intended audience.

The same concept may require different explanations for different readers.

Examples:

Consumer Audience:

> Sign in once and use multiple websites.

Technical Audience:

> Federated authentication allows a single identity to access multiple services.

Architect Audience:

> OpenWebAuth provides decentralized single sign-on across independently operated domains.

Vocabulary should match the reader's needs, not the author's expertise.

## Favor Concrete Language

Prefer concrete language whenever possible.

Concrete language is easier to understand and easier to remember.

Prefer:

> Create a community.

Over:

> Facilitate community engagement.

Prefer:

> Move your identity.

Over:

> Enable identity portability.

Abstract language is appropriate when discussing abstract concepts.

Otherwise, prefer language readers can immediately visualize and understand.

## Explain Why It Matters

Facts without context have limited value.

Whenever practical, explain:

* why something matters
* who it affects
* potential benefits
* potential drawbacks
* practical consequences

Readers should understand both what something is and why they should care.

## Use Examples to Build Understanding

Concepts are easier to understand when connected to concrete examples.

When introducing a new idea, consider whether a practical example, comparison, or analogy would help readers understand it more quickly.

Examples should clarify the concept, not distract from it.

Prefer examples that:

* reflect real-world usage
* match the audience's experience
* illustrate practical implications
* reduce ambiguity

A well-chosen example often communicates more effectively than several paragraphs of explanation.

Use examples to improve understanding, not to fill space.

## Distinguish Related Concepts

Many misunderstandings occur because related concepts are treated as identical.

When two concepts are commonly confused, explain both the relationship and the distinction.

Examples include:

* ownership vs authorship
* account vs identity
* authentication vs authorization
* protocol vs application
* website vs community

Readers should leave with a clearer understanding of where concepts overlap and where they differ.

Clarifying distinctions often provides more value than introducing additional concepts.

## Define Before Debating

Before discussing benefits, drawbacks, comparisons, or opinions, establish a clear definition of the subject.

Whenever practical, follow this progression:

1. What it is
2. How it works
3. Why it exists
4. Benefits
5. Limitations
6. Comparisons and alternatives

Readers cannot meaningfully evaluate something they do not yet understand.

Definitions should come before debates.

Understanding should come before conclusions.

## Teach Decision-Making, Not Just Facts

Information is useful.

Judgment is more useful.

Whenever practical, help readers understand:

* when a solution is appropriate
* when it may not be appropriate
* relevant tradeoffs
* practical consequences
* factors that influence the decision

The goal is not merely to transfer information.

The goal is to help readers make informed decisions.

Facts explain what something is.

Context and tradeoffs help readers determine what to do with that knowledge.


## Professional But Human

Write as an experienced practitioner.

Avoid:

* hype
* exaggerated claims
* clickbait
* unnecessary marketing language
* corporate jargon

Prefer:

* direct statements
* practical explanations
* measured conclusions
* real-world examples

The goal is credibility, not excitement.

## Confident But Accurate

State facts clearly.

Avoid unnecessary hedging.

Avoid overstating certainty.

Use confidence when supported by facts.

Use nuance when nuance is required.

## Respect the Reader

Assume readers are intelligent.

Do not talk down to them.

Do not over-explain simple concepts.

Do not assume expertise they may not possess.

Explain specialized terminology when it first becomes important.

## Structure Matters

Organize information so readers can quickly find what they need.

Use:

* descriptive headings
* logical progression
* short introductory sections
* lists when appropriate

Headings should communicate content, not create curiosity.

Prefer:

> Identity Cloning and Redundancy

Over:

> A Powerful Feature You May Not Know About

## One Idea Per Section

Each section should have a clear purpose.

Avoid mixing unrelated concepts.

If a topic requires a significant shift in focus, create a new section.

## Use Lists When They Improve Readability

Lists improve:

* scanning
* comprehension
* maintenance
* future editing

Use lists when they make information easier to understand.

Do not use lists when normal prose communicates the idea more naturally.

## Infrastructure Perspective

When writing about systems, focus on:

* capabilities
* relationships
* tradeoffs
* outcomes

Do not focus exclusively on features.

Readers often care less about what a system contains and more about what a system enables.

## Reality Before Theory

Explain how something actually works before discussing future possibilities or theoretical designs.

Readers should understand:

1. Current reality
2. Current capabilities
3. Limitations
4. Future possibilities

Do not present aspirations as existing functionality.

## Simplicity on the Surface

Complex systems should be explained from the user's perspective first.

Begin with:

* what the user experiences
* what the user can do
* why it matters

Introduce implementation details only when they improve understanding.

The existence of complexity does not require exposing complexity.

## Preferred Outcome

A successful article causes the reader to think:

> I understand this topic.

Not:

> The author is knowledgeable.

Understanding is the objective.

Everything else is secondary.
